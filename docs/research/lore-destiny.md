# Destiny lore study: what Worlds Infinite can learn from it

Research notes on how Destiny (2014) and Destiny 2 (2017-2025) write and deliver their lore, read
against Worlds Infinite's premise system (`docs/world-premises.md`, `src/content/premises/FORMAT.md`,
`src/app/journal.js`). It was written in response to the owner's feedback that our fragments
read too cryptic and mystical, and that they want grounded, easy-to-follow text with real
content and stakes.

Quotes are kept short (one line each) and everything else is paraphrased. Source works are named
here for reference only. As with all research docs, no Destiny names or terms go into content files.

**The short version.** Destiny is known for being cryptic, but most of the lore its fans love
is the opposite. It is letters, diaries, debriefs, interrogations and a gunslinger's memoir,
written in plain sentences by named people who want something. The mythology is huge, but each
page is small: one person, one moment, one thing they know. The famous cryptic stretches are
either deliberate voices (a dragon, a god, a scripture) or the parts players complained about.

## 1. Writing techniques

### 1.1 Every page is a document with an author, an addressee and a reason

Almost every good entry answers three questions in its first lines: who is writing, to whom, and
why now.

| Entry | Who writes, to whom, why | What it carries |
|---|---|---|
| *Books of Sorrow*, "Predators" (Taken King, 2015) | A young girl, Xi Ro, carves a list of things that can kill you on her home world. | The origin of a god-race of the setting, told as a child's survival list. It opens: *"MOON WAVES. My sister Aurash is afraid of moon waves."* |
| *Calcified Fragments: Curiosity* | Aurash writes to her sister Sathona as a birthday present, after two years of research. | A plain statement of a huge secret: *"We aren't native to the Fundament. Our ancient ancestors came here to hide."* |
| *The Black Armory Papers*, "Final Entry" | A dying gunsmith writes to her daughter. | The daughter's mind was moved into a machine body without her consent, at her mother's order. The emotional line is *"I got to love you twice."* |
| *The Man They Call Cayde* (Forsaken, 2018) | A Hunter leader writes a journal to his (possibly imagined) son, hidden in treasure caches. | His fear of having his memory wiped and of who he'd become: *"Find the path to your best self and walk it."* |
| *Ghost Fragment: Skyline* (D1) | A military after-action debrief from a robot security unit. | A Golden Age weapons lab under attack, told through unit numbers and casualty counts. |
| *Ghost Fragment: Thorn 4* | A redacted transcript between a Guardian and someone else's Ghost. | The villain explains himself. The Ghost answers: *"A man can be killed."* |
| *Wayfinder's Voyage* (Season of the Lost, 2021) | Transcripts of a captive schemer talking to the player. | Political manipulation, in clear modern speech: was the secret kept *"to protect Crow from himself? Or ... to protect themselves from Crow?"* |
| *Ghost Fragment: Cayde-6* (D1) | A database query log of Cayde searching the archive while bored. | Comedy, plus real world facts in the archive's dry replies. |
| *Tarrabah* (weapon lore) | An archivist who saves words for extinct animals. | Earth's ecology and earlier collapses: *"As other Guardians save humans, I save words."* |

**Lesson.** A fragment that does not know who wrote it ends up cryptic. A fragment written by
someone to someone for a reason has to be concrete, because that person is trying to get something
across.

### 1.2 Cosmic truths through small personal moments

The *Books of Sorrow* tell how a whole species became a death cult that eats worlds. The way in
is three sisters: the little one who wants to be a knight, the clever one who finds the truth, the
ambitious one who wants to be a Mother. The book only reaches the cosmic bargain (verse IX, "The
Bargain") after we know them. The god's offer is in plain terms: power and eternal life now, and a
parasite whose hunger grows with your power.

Other examples:
- The giant fact that Exo minds are reset and rebuilt lands in *Cayde's journal* as one man telling
  his future self how many times he'd allow himself to be rebooted.
- The Golden Age's god-machine experiments land in the *Black Armory Papers* as a mother's apology.
- *The Last Word* (D1) tells the story of a legendary duel from the point of view of a friend who
  waited at the ravine, heard the shots stop, and saw the dead man's Ghost come back alone. The
  prose is spare: *"What was done was done."*
- *Ghost Stories* (Shadowkeep) uses dead Ghosts: the last words of a soul-companion over a fallen
  fireteam, with timestamps. The doomed raid on a god is told through a team arguing about whether
  to go.

**Lesson.** The truth should hurt a particular person. The reader remembers "her mother moved her
mind into a machine" long after forgetting any cosmology.

### 1.3 Plain declarative sentences carry the facts

Even the mythic books state facts in short, flat sentences. "The Flower Game" (*Unveiling*,
Shadowkeep) explains the cosmic struggle by teaching the rules of a cellular automaton first:
*"A living flower with less than two living neighbors is cut off. It dies."* Only after the rules
are clear does it say the cosmic powers *"learned those rules, because they were those rules."* The
mystery is in what the facts imply, not in the wording.

When Destiny writes well, it hides a truth by:
- **Who knows it** (only one sister did the research),
- **Who is lying** (the official doctrine, the "Timid Truth", says their people are prey forever),
- **What is missing** (redacted names, lost pages, a dead narrator),

and never by vague wording.

### 1.4 Unreliable narrators and the late recontextualisation

- The *Books of Sorrow* are told by the conquerors themselves, mostly the king, Oryx. Readers took
  them as true for years. In *The Witch Queen* (2022), seven years later, the game showed that
  the whispers that led the sisters to the gods were planted by a greater enemy, and that the
  "good" god had been about to come to their world. The scripture was true as far as the
  narrator knew, but it had been engineered.
- *Truth to Power* (Forsaken) and *Unveiling* are openly self-interested: a hidden AI confesses
  mid-letter who she is (*"I am Medusa, survivor of the Golden Age..."*), and the arch-enemy pitches
  its philosophy in its own voice (*"Imagine a world without me."*).
- The villain in *Thorn* calls himself a force of nature; the Ghost's reply cuts him back to a man.

**Lesson.** The bias belongs to a character with a motive, and a later document answers it. The
reader gets two clear claims that contradict each other, not one vague one.

### 1.5 Several voices on the same event

The same legend gets told in several registers:
- *Dredgen Yor and Shin Malphur*: the villain's redacted transcript (Thorn), the hero's own memoir
  (The Last Word), a weapon's one-liner (*"Yours... not mine."*), and later a grim follow-up in
  *A Drifter's Gambit* where the hero hunts the villain's imitators.
- *The death of a god* (Oryx): his own scripture, the warlock Toland's commentary, the player's
  campaign, and a Ghost's report.
- *Felwinter* (*The Liar*, Season of the Worthy): an Iron Lord's life from waking up with no memory,
  interleaved with machine logs that show who he really was. The logs and the memoir agree on the
  events and disagree on what they meant.

### 1.6 Varied registers stop the lore from all sounding the same

Within one setting you get a child's list, a birthday letter, scripture verses, a Western memoir,
military debriefs, database logs, sermons and propaganda, a gunsmith's notes, a sea captain's log
(*Captain's Log*, unlocked weekly through the Presage mission), a cheeky skull (*Skull of Dire
Ahamkara*), and a bored Hunter's search history. Variety of form does a lot of the work: readers
can tell at a glance what kind of document they are holding and how far to trust it.

### 1.7 Where Destiny goes poetic, and why it works there

Poetic or archaic writing appears in specific voices:
- **Scripture and liturgy** ("Court of Oryx": a hymn listing the god-king's court, *"Who walked in
  front of him? His daughters..."*). It is a list of real names and roles, and right after it
  Toland explains the point plainly: *"Oryx depends on His Court... Do you see why?"*
- **Wish-dragons** (Ahamkara armour lore). They whisper *"O bearer mine..."* because they are
  tempters, and the flattery is the threat.
- **Cosmic entities** speaking in their own voice (*Unveiling*). They are rare, short, and come
  late, after hundreds of plain pages.
- **Flavour one-liners** with a named source: *"If there is beauty in destruction, why not also in
  its delivery?"* (Gjallarhorn), *"To rend one's enemies is to see them not as equals, but
  objects..."* (Thorn, attributed to a Book of Sorrow).

Rule of thumb: **the poetry is characterisation**, a voice that would talk that way, and it is
almost always framed by plain text or a clear speaker. Mystical prose without a speaker is what
reads as cryptic.

### 1.8 Humour and warmth

Cayde's jokes, the database search log, the cheeky skull mocking stuffier ones (*"What kind of
talking skull would address its host that way?"*). The darkest setting in the series has light
pages, and those make the dark ones land.

## 2. Delivery and UX techniques

### 2.1 Where the lore lives

| Vehicle | How it works | Worlds Infinite analogue |
|---|---|---|
| **Grimoire cards** (D1) | Earned for kills, missions and finds, but readable only on the website/app. Heavily criticised (see 4). | Never put the payload somewhere separate from where it was found. |
| **Dead Ghosts** | Hidden corpses of Guardians' companions in out-of-the-way spots. Each holds a page. Shadowkeep made them a quest: drops point toward a location (*"Find a dead Ghost in the Lunar Battlegrounds."*). | Ruins/sites holding personal last words. Hints that name a region. |
| **Calcified Fragments** | 50 crystals across the Dreadnaught, each a verse of *Books of Sorrow*, in order. | A numbered book whose pages are scattered over one region or site. |
| **Ghost scans** | Look at an object; your companion explains it in a plain sentence or two, sometimes with a second character chiming in (Taken King onward). | A plain "what you are looking at" layer on every site (our `surface`). |
| **Lore books** (D2, Forsaken onward) | Named books in Triumphs > Lore, with a fixed number of pages (e.g. *The Liar* 13, *Marasenna* 27, *Black Armory Papers* 18, *Ghost Stories* 23). Locked pages are visible as locked, so you know how much is missing. | Group fragments into named books with "4 of 9 pages". |
| **Page unlock routes** | Kill counts with a weapon (*The Liar*), hidden caches (*Cayde's journal*), weekly mission runs (*Captain's Log*), Hive scrolls in a dungeon (*Inquisition of the Damned*), quest steps, seasonal triumphs. | Different books open in different ways: reading places, digging sites, history turning. |
| **Item lore tabs** | Every legendary/exotic has flavour text plus a page of lore. A weapon's page is often a short story about someone else entirely. | Attach one-liners and short stories to units, techs, rulers, artefacts. |
| **Transcripts / radio** | NPC dialogue during weekly missions, archived as readable transcripts. | History events that write their own documents into the journal. |
| **Living places** | The Dreaming City runs on a three-week curse loop: quiet, spreading corruption, near-overrun, reset. Missions and chests change with the week. | Map zones that visibly change as the premise's storyline advances. |
| **Physical props** | Collector's editions: Cayde's annotated *Treasure Island*, Clovis Bray's journal. Marginalia showed a sadder Cayde than the cutscenes. | Annotated documents: an official text with a reader's notes in the margin. |

### 2.2 Signposting what exists and what is missing

- The D2 lore book UI shows the shape of the book before you have it: title, cover, locked page
  slots. Knowing there are 13 pages makes people go after the 13th.
- Quest text says where to look in plain words ("in the Lunar Battlegrounds"). Even mystery hooks
  are concrete: *"Did that Knight look familiar?"* is a question about a real enemy you just met.
- Page titles promise a story: *The Liar*'s pages run "The Beginning", "The Name", "The Assailant"
  ... "The Lure", "The Mistake". The last title tells you there's a mistake coming.
- Weekly releases (*Captain's Log*, seasonal stories since Season of the Chosen) gave the
  community a reason to come back together and compare notes.

### 2.3 How fans assemble it

The fan site Ishtar Collective archives every entry, tags it by character, place and event, and
builds timelines and character hubs. YouTubers such as Byf and Myelin Games read entries aloud and
connect them. Bungie's narrative team has spoken of mining old lore so new stories stay consistent,
and of writing the main beats so everyone gets them while leaving deep cuts for veterans.

**Lesson.** The journal should do the Ishtar job for the player: index fragments by person,
place, event and truth, put them on a timeline, and show what is still missing.

### 2.4 Mystery hooks and payoffs across years

- The Hive's origin (2015) was reframed in 2022.
- The Darkness and the Witness ran from the first game's opening line to *The Final Shape* (2024).
- The Dreadnaught, Rasputin (an AI first mentioned in D1 grimoire and finished in Season of the
  Seraph, 2022) and Cayde's past were all seeded long before they were resolved.

The trick is that each step pays off something concrete (a name, a face, a motive), even when the
larger question stays open. Readers can put up with a long mystery as long as each page tells them
something.

## 3. Structural patterns

1. **Book = one narrator + one arc.** Most D2 books are 10-25 pages by one or two voices, with a
   beginning, turn and end. *The Liar*: amnesia, identity, discovery, mistake. *Black Armory
   Papers*: a family business, a war, a death, a confession.
2. **Interleaved counter-voice.** A memoir interleaved with logs (*The Liar*), a letter with
   corrupted data headers (*Truth to Power*), a fairy tale with footnotes that pick it apart
   ("Gardener and Winnower").
3. **The plain-statement page.** Somewhere in each book one page states the secret outright, with
   no hedging ("We aren't native to the Fundament"). The mystery is whether you trust who said it.
4. **Concrete before cosmic.** Rules before theology ("The Flower Game"); a child's list before the
   god-bargain; a debrief before the Collapse.
5. **People recur across books.** Cayde, Toland, Eris, Shin, Ana Bray turn up in many books under
   different lights. Readers follow people, not concepts.
6. **Objects carry stories about their owners.** A gun's lore is a story about the person who
   carried it, made it, or was killed by it.
7. **Events have documents.** Each big in-game event gets a debrief, a sermon, an enemy's account
   and a personal letter, written by people who were there.
8. **The world shows the lore.** The Dreaming City's loop, the Moon's nightmares, Hive scrolls in a
   dungeon: what is true is also visible on the map.
9. **Episodic release.** Seasons tell a story weekly. Each week adds a transcript, a mission and a
   few lore pages, and ends on a question.

## 4. Criticisms and how the writers answered them

| Criticism | What happened | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Lore outside the game** | D1 kept its story on grimoire cards readable only on the website/app. Players did missions without knowing why and learned the reason afterwards on a card. | Destiny 2 dropped the grimoire. World lead Steve Cotton said they wanted to "put the lore in the game": scannables, lore tabs on items, lore books in Triumphs, characters in the world. |
| **Story that refuses to explain** | The vanilla D1 campaign became a meme for the Stranger's line, *"I don't even have time to explain why I don't have time to explain."* Characters hinted and left. | Taken King and later expansions gave characters clear goals and plain talk (Cayde, Eris, Toland's "Do you see why?"). Ghost scans explain the objects you look at. |
| **Unexplained terms** | *Lightfall* (2023) was widely criticised because its central object, the Veil, was barely explained beyond the word "paracausal", and characters talked as if the player already knew things. The best story arrived after the campaign, in logs. | *The Final Shape* (2024) was received much better: it spelled out the stakes, gave the enemy a clear goal, and paid off old threads in the main story instead of in side texts. |
| **Lore too hard to follow** | Lore books are rich but fragmented; many players relied on YouTube summaries. | Bungie's later approach was to make sure the main beats are in the playable story, explained "as if for new players", and keep the deep lore as optional depth. |
| **Lore gated behind grind** | Some pages needed hundreds of weapon kills or rare drops. Players complained when lore was locked behind bugged or retired content. | Seasonal triumphs later became legacy and opened to everyone. Archives kept the text available. |

**What this means for us.** The owner's complaint (too cryptic, too mystical) is the same one
Destiny got, and Destiny answered it in the same way each time: named characters, plain
statements, and the essentials delivered in the main experience. Mythic prose stayed, but as one
voice among many.

## 5. Takeaways for Worlds Infinite

Mapped to our data (`fragments`, `sites`, `storylines`, the journal). Points 2, 3 and 10 back up
rules already in `docs/style.md`. The rest are structural or delivery changes that the style
guide does not cover yet: authors, books, recurring people, event-linked accounts and signposting.

1. **Every fragment has an author, an addressee, and a reason.** Add (or generate) `who`, `to`,
   and `why` for each fragment: "a mill-owner's widow, writing to the magistrate, asking why her
   son's regiment came back changed". The text then follows naturally. A fragment that can't say
   who wrote it is the one that ends up cryptic.
2. **Default register: plain and concrete.** One to three short declarative sentences with nouns
   you could draw: numbers, names, places, prices, dates. Ban unattributed mysticism. Poetic
   wording is allowed only when the source is a hymn, a prophecy, a tempter or a mad heretic, and
   then it must still list concrete things (as the "Court of Oryx" hymn does).
3. **Each truth gets one plain-statement fragment.** At least one reliable core fragment says the
   truth outright in everyday words ("The red stones are made from prisoners. I watched them do
   it."). The puzzle is whether to believe it, not what it means. The current `balanced_circle`
   "dying wright's confession" and "cell wall count" fragments are the right model; "the world
   sleeps on a sleeping god" is the kind to cut or reframe.
4. **Cosmic truth through one person's loss.** Every truth needs 1-2 fragments where a named,
   generated person (from `{person}`, a ruler, a history figure) pays for it: a son, a village, a
   promise. Give each truth a "so what": who dies, who profits, what happens if it comes out.
5. **Group fragments into books with page counts.** Generate 1-3 "books" per premise: a single
   narrator's serial account (6-12 pages) scattered across holders, with page titles that promise
   an arc ("The Commission", "The Count", "The Furnace", "The Mistake"). Show "Book: *The Wright's
   Ledger*, 4 of 9 pages" in the journal, with locked slots visible.
6. **Several voices on the same real event.** Tie fragments to actual events from the simulated
   history (a battle, a plague, a founding, a purge): the official chronicle, a soldier's diary,
   the enemy's account, a temple's sermon. Same date, same place, different claims. Contradiction
   between clear accounts is the puzzle we want, not fog.
7. **Bias belongs to someone with a motive.** Instead of `bias: "garbled"` as flavour, say why the
   text is wrong: the crown wrote it to justify the borders; the order wants recruits; the heretic
   lost his brother. Later fragments should answer earlier ones explicitly ("The Academy primer
   says the stone is a metaphor. The man who wrote that primer ran the Twelfth Workshop.").
8. **Recurring characters.** Let 2-4 generated people per premise author several fragments across
   the world (the investigator, the true believer, the survivor, the official). Readers follow
   people; the journal should list fragments by person as well as by truth.
9. **Signpost concretely.** Locked site layers and missing pages should say where to look and
   what kind of thing is needed in plain words ("A soldier's letter from the Ashfield garrison
   mentions this place"; "Two more pages of this book are held in {realm}"). A hook should be a
   specific question ("Why were the prisoners counted in pebbles?"), not an atmosphere.
10. **Explain every coined term on first sight.** Avoid the *Lightfall* problem: if a fragment uses
    a premise word ("the current", "the Twelfth Workshop", "chalk"), the first fragment a player
    is likely to find must gloss it plainly. Prefer ordinary words to coined ones.
11. **Lore on things people already look at.** Give units, techs, artefacts, rulers and wonders a
    one-line flavour quote with an attribution ("— a quartermaster of {realm}, {year}") and, for
    important ones, a short story. This is the item-lore-tab pattern, and it puts the premise
    into the parts of the UI people already click.
12. **Vary the document form.** Rotate between ledger, letter, debrief, interrogation, child's
    rhyme, sermon, wanted poster, court transcript, travel guide, recipe, graffiti. Label the form
    in the UI (as `journal.js` labels source and bias) so trust can be read at a glance.
13. **Let the world show the truth.** Premise storylines should change map zones and site states
    over the simulated history (the Dreaming City pattern): a corruption that spreads, a wall that
    goes up, a valley that goes dead. A fragment that explains a visible change lands much harder
    than one that explains an abstraction.
14. **Pay off in steps, and reframe at the end.** Each site layer and each book page should tell
    the reader something new and concrete. The `revelation` layer should reframe an earlier
    trusted source (the *Books of Sorrow* move): what you believed was true as far as the writer
    knew, and here is who set them up.
15. **Allow warmth and humour.** One or two light fragments per premise (a bored clerk's note, a
    sarcastic tavern tale) make the grim ones land and keep the journal readable.
