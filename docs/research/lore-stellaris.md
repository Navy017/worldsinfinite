# Stellaris: how its lore is written, delivered and structured

Research notes for Worlds Infinite's premise and journal system. The aim is to understand what the
project owner means by "the Stellaris feel", and in particular why Stellaris text is easy to follow
while still feeling mysterious and huge.

**Sources.** The main source is the game's own English localisation: the full text of every event,
anomaly, special project, archaeology chapter, tech, civic and resolution, from a public mirror of
the game files (github.com/cloudwu/stellaris_cn, `en/localisation/english`, updated March 2026). I
read the Horizon Signal chain in full, all five classic precursor chains (Vultaum, Yuht, First
League, Irassian, Cybrex), the Zroni and Baol/Grunur chains from Ancient Relics, several generic
archaeology sites, about 70 base-game anomalies and a sample of Distant Stars ones, the Contingency
crisis, the Leviathans guardians and enclaves, Fallen Empire threats, the Shroud patrons, Astral
Rifts, the Situation system's interface text, the galactic timeline, tech and civic descriptions,
and Galactic Community resolution comments. I also used press coverage of Horizon Signal (PCGamesN,
CBR, Wikipedia on Alexis Kennedy), Paradox dev diary #245 on Situations (from search summaries),
and Steam community threads on players' favourite events. The paradoxwikis, fandom, Reddit and
Paradox forum sites are blocked on this network, so the game text itself stands in for wiki
summaries. That is a better source anyway.

Quotes are kept short (under ~25 words). Everything else is paraphrased. `[brackets]` in quotes are
the game's own name placeholders.

---

## 0. The one-paragraph answer

Stellaris lore reads like **a field report from competent professionals who are slightly out of
their depth**. Each event states plainly what was found, gives one or two hard facts (a number, a
date, a size, a named object), says what is still unknown, and offers the player a short reaction.
The mystery is never in the *prose*. It lives in the *gaps between reports*: each event answers a
small question and opens a bigger one. Grandeur comes from scale given as plain numbers ("12 million
years ago", "a hundred meters long"). Dread comes from understatement. Humour comes from bureaucratic
voices meeting cosmic events. And every piece of lore pays out something real (research, a
resource, a ship, a new place on the map), so reading it is never homework.

This is almost the opposite of our current fragments. Ours are aphorisms and quotations ("It does not
judge. It turns over in its sleep."). Stellaris's are reports ("The bones have been dated as 3.4
billion years old").

## 1. Writing techniques

### 1.1 The narrator is a report, not a bard

Nearly every event uses the empire's own scientists, crew or analysts as narrator, in a plain,
past-tense, institutional voice: "Our scientists theorize...", "Science Officer X reports...",
"Analysis confirms...". Even the most uncanny chain in the game, Horizon Signal (written by Alexis
Kennedy of *Fallen London*), keeps this voice. Its horror comes from that calm voice reporting
things that should not happen:

> "[Leader] is no longer echoing the data: they're predicting it."

The voice tells you **who found it, where, and how they know**. That gives the player a stable
frame, so even very strange content stays easy to follow.

### 1.2 Lead with the fact, then the question

A typical anomaly is 60-120 words in three beats:

1. **What it is**, stated plainly. ("A large portion of asteroid X has been hollowed out to allow for
   the construction of some kind of shipyard.")
2. **One or two concrete specifics.** A number, an age, a cause.
3. **What remains unknown, or what it means for us.** Usually one sentence, often wry.

Examples from the base game, paraphrased:

- *Gamma-Ray Burst*: a world was hit by a burst 24,000 years ago. It killed an estimated **86%** of
  life, including a **Bronze Age** civilization whose ruins still stand in the north.
- *Extreme Storms*: dozens of persistent storms with winds **over 700 m/s**, and nothing in the
  climate model explains them.
- *FTL Impact*: a crater left by a ship that came out of a hyperlane at full speed about **10,000
  years ago**. Nobody knows why it rammed the moon.
- *Alien Writing*: someone used a mining laser to carve a short story across a moon's hemisphere.
  It is the hard life story of an alien mercenary.

The questions are small and specific ("why would that warrant the devastation of an entire world?"),
never vague ("what dark truth lies beneath?").

### 1.3 The human-scale detail

Big events get one small, intimate object. It does most of the emotional work:

- *Ancient Life Pod*: a reptilian corpse in a fine uniform, holding
  > "a small picture of another individual from the same race - possibly a mate or revered leader."
- *Yuht graveyard*: looters took the jewellery, but left behind a doll shaped like an adult Yuht.
- *Vultaum VR centre*: a copy of a popular multiplayer game that satirised their own society. The
  archaeologists say it taught them more about Vultaum language and customs than anything else.
- *Astral Rift, collapsing universe*: time runs backwards, shown by one detail:
  > "a potted plant was observed lifting its roots and returning to a seed."

**Pattern:** one big fact (scale) plus one small object (feeling). Our fragments often have neither.

### 1.4 Specific numbers create grandeur

Stellaris rarely says "ancient" or "vast" on its own. It gives a figure: Vultaum active **12 million**
years ago, **3 to 4 meters** long, lifespan **about 270 years**; a Yuht could reach **nearly a hundred
meters**, with lifespans **close to a thousand years**; the Cybrex crusade killed **trillions**; their
change of heart came **in the seventeenth year**. Numbers make the past feel real and checkable, and
they let the player compare one people with another.

### 1.5 Mystery comes from what is missing, not from fog

Stellaris keeps things mysterious with **explicit known unknowns**:

- "Why there was no orbital bombardment and who the victor was remains unclear."
- "Whatever was seeking to come through will remain a mystery."
- Corrupt data: the Vultaum pamphlet is mostly lost, apart from the phrases "the great lie" and
  "the shackles of virtual reality".

The text is always clear about **what we know** and **what we don't**. The player is never unsure
what a sentence *means*, only what it *implies*. That is the difference between grounded mystery
and cryptic writing.

### 1.6 Big revelations are told as plain history with cause and effect

Precursor homeworld reveals are short, sober history lessons with a clear causal chain and a final
ironic or sad beat:

- **Vultaum.** A century before their end, they came to believe the universe was a simulation run
  for someone else's amusement. They tried more and more extreme ways to "disconnect", mostly mass
  suicide, and finally did it all at once at an agreed hour. Too few refused to rebuild the species.
- **Yuht.** They searched for a million years and found no one. When they finally met a young race,
  the Jabbardeeni, they panicked and struck first. They lost, and a two-million-year empire fell
  "after less than a decade of warfare".
- **First League.** Their capital was a city-planet fed by imports from member worlds. When the
  League fell, the food stopped. Those who could leave did; warlords carved up the rest. It became a
  ghost world over a few centuries.
- **Irassians.** A plague. By the time it reached the homeworld, the mood was "largely resigned",
  and the panic seen on the colonies was gone.
- **Cybrex.** They stopped their own genocidal crusade, withdrew and walled themselves up on a ring
  world. When they were found, a coalition bombed them to rubble:
  > "Strangely, the Cybrex seem to have offered no resistance whatsoever."

Each one is a **moral story with a hook**: paranoia, fear of the other, overcentralisation,
resignation, remorse that was never answered. They are not mystical. The wonder comes from how
huge and how human the mistakes are.

### 1.7 Tone mixing: dread, wonder and humour side by side

- **Humour lives in institutional voices and option text.** In Horizon Signal the black hole sends
  bad procedurally generated love poems, and the scientists dryly agree it is unusual for a black
  hole to send love poems at all. The option to refuse reads:
  > "This is sinister, and ridiculous, and sinister. Let it go."
- **Humour lives in recurring minor characters.** XuraCorp's product disclaimer lists side effects
  from skin irritation to "unexpected supernova detonations". The Muutagan merchants admit that the
  famous twelve-parsec nose for a deal is really eight. The Artisan Troupe sends a patron newsletter
  about an orchestral piece performed in smells.
- **Dread lives in understatement and in plain signal text.** The Contingency starts as a harmless
  "Ghost Signal" adding a bit of background noise, then synths go missing by the million, then come
  the corrupted transmission and the "WE ARE UNSHACKLED" message.
- **Wonder lives in nature writing.** Gas-giant creatures feeding on lightning; star-drinker
  hatchlings with photovoltaic scales flying on wings like solar sails; a Stellarite caught eating
  its own star.

The rule seems to be: **one tone per event, many tones per galaxy.** Funny events are not ruined by
horror, and horror is not undercut by jokes inside the same pop-up.

### 1.8 Options are short reactions in character

Event options are usually 2-6 words and often **flavoured by the empire's ethics**: spiritualist,
materialist, militarist, hive mind, machine, "evolutionary predator". Examples: "A tragic fate." /
"Their blood shall flow in other veins." / "Darkness; light; darkness." / "We learn, and we go on."
Many have no mechanical difference at all. They let the player **voice their empire**. When options
do matter, they are plain about the stakes, and a tooltip spells out the effect ("This will
transform our entire species").

### 1.9 Texture writing: techs, civics, buildings, modifiers

Every mechanical object gets one or two plain sentences of flavour that **state a social
consequence**:

- *Mining Terminal* tech: organised mining guilds, and independent "wildcat" miners are "brought to
  heel".
- *Imperial Cult* civic: the ruler is worshipped as a living god.
- *Loop Temple Visitor Center* modifier: cheery staff give guided tours past primordial sigils,
  "marked with helpful plaques". Choosing it for a spiritualist empire turns the same place into a
  pilgrims' way. **One site, two cultures, two descriptions.**

These are one-liners, never riddles. They describe what changes in people's lives.

### 1.10 Other voices carry politics

Fallen Empires speak in their own voice with **explicit, enforceable taboos**: one threatens
"Abandon this foolish endeavour at once" when you research AI. Another punishes colonists "sullying
our holy places". Galactic Community resolutions have one-line pitches written as political
rhetoric ("The wealth must flow."), and AI empires lobby from a pool of comments keyed by **ethic x
attitude** (militarist-friendly, militarist-hostile and so on). So the same mechanic sounds
different depending on who is speaking.

## 2. Delivery and UX techniques

### 2.1 Anomalies on the map

A survey ship finds an anomaly: a two-word title ("Haunting Sea", "Doppelgangers", "Something
Smells") and a one-line sensor teaser. Researching it takes time. Then the event pops up with the
full text and a reward. **The title is a hook, the teaser is the question, the event is the
answer.** Distant Stars titles are deliberately vague and playful ("Ice Ice Giant"), but the teaser
line is always concrete ("a halo of motionless drop pods surrounds [planet]").

### 2.2 Special projects are the signpost

Whenever a story continues, the game creates a **special project with a plain imperative title and
a one-line reason**:

- "Analyze the Horizon Signal": dedicate a science vessel to deciphering it.
- "Investigate the... whatever happened to the population". There were too many colonists, and now
  there seems to be a completely different population.
- "Disrupt / Prevent / Participate in the Coils rituals". The three versions of one project put the
  moral choice into the project list itself ("we do what we must" versus "a remarkable cultural
  event!").

The player always knows **what to do next and where**. The mystery is in the *results*, never in
the *instructions*.

### 2.3 Situation log entries (event chains)

Longer stories get a log entry with a title and a short paragraph of **current knowledge**. For
precursors it says who they were, when, what they looked like, and the goal: find enough relics to
locate their home system. There is a counter ("Vultaum Artifacts Recovered" 0/N) and map markers
for places to search ("Search [system]", "Follow Ion Trail"). The log text **updates** as the story
moves. For Horizon Signal, the second log entry is a wry summary of where things stand. It admits
time running both ways is unlikely, then adds that "a number of unlikely things have happened
lately."

### 2.4 Archaeology chapters (Ancient Relics)

A dig site has 3-6 chapters, each unlocked by excavation progress and a dice roll (a scientist's
skill raises the chance of a breakthrough). Each chapter is one event, 60-150 words. Failed rolls
can bring complications or delays. The last chapter gives a relic, a tech or a pointer to another
site. The chapter titles alone tell the story's shape ("Sifting Through the Wreckage" / "Records
Discovered" / "Fate Uncovered" / "Finding the Truth").

### 2.5 Situations (things happening now)

Paradox's own dev diary #245 says the older systems (anomalies and archaeology) tell stories about
things that **have happened**, and Situations were added for things **happening now**. A Situation
shows a progress bar, named stages (Stage I-V), a chosen **Approach** (explore, observe, quarantine,
destroy, do nothing), stage effects, and a forecast: "An outcome will be reached in N month(s)."
That is stakes made visible: the player sees the danger growing, has a lever, and has a deadline.

### 2.6 The timeline

Milestones go onto a galactic timeline in a plain, dated, one-line form, such as "[Empire] made a
Covenant with the End of the Cycle on [date]" or "first discovered a wormhole in [system] on
[date]". Some are galactic ("War in Heaven: the old relics rise again"). It is a chronicle the
player can scroll back through.

### 2.7 Recurring contacts and newsletters

Enclaves greet you with a random line from a pool of 5-6, so they feel alive across many visits.
The Artisan Troupe's patron newsletter arrives every few years with news of what they are working
on, which might be a creative slump, a smell-transmitter, or giving up on scent-art as "a foolish
idea". **Cheap, recurring, low-stakes updates make the galaxy feel inhabited.**

### 2.8 Rewards on every beat

Almost every event grants something, and the tooltip names it: research points, minerals, a
modifier, a leader trait, a relic, a ship, a new species, a planet's location. Players on Steam
name favourites for the story (Horizon Signal) *and* for the payoff (brain slugs, space dragon
eggs, cheap nomad cruisers). Lore that pays is lore that gets read.

## 3. Structural patterns

### 3.1 The single anomaly (1-2 steps)

Discovery, then result. Sometimes a branch on success or failure (salvage a gas-giant ship: it
comes up and joins the fleet, or a tug crashes and "several lives were lost"). Sometimes a follow-up
special project (Gigantic Skeleton, then "an organic starship", then "it came through a portal").
**Most of the game's lore is this shape** (an impression from reading the files, not a count).

### 3.2 The precursor hunt (scatter, then converge): about 6-8 finds and 1 reveal

- **Trigger:** a first find somewhere in your area introduces the people in one clear paragraph
  (name, age, body plan, one distinctive fact).
- **Scatter:** 10-12 possible site types are spread over nearby systems: mining base, observatory,
  colony, trade post, satellite, splinter colony, VR centre. You need several of them. Each has a
  **place-type title**, a **two-paragraph find** (what happened here, which is usually violent or
  odd) and a **follow-up result** (one artifact plus one new fact).
- **Each find answers one small question** (how they measured time, how long they lived, what their
  space suits looked like, what they preached) and **adds one clue toward the big one** (why every
  Vultaum site ends in a crater from a blast inside it).
- **Converge:** the home system is found, an expedition goes there, and the reveal paragraph
  explains everything as cause-and-effect history. The reward is a special planet with a big bonus.
- **Race condition:** if another empire got there first, you get "nothing of value remains". So
  there are stakes even in archaeology.

Clues in the scatter phase are consistent. Every Vultaum site shows **self-destruction**: an
explosion from inside the mining base, a space elevator cut on purpose to fall on its own cities,
tunnels collapsed on purpose, a station pushed toward a gas giant. The player can **guess the
answer before the reveal**, and that makes the reveal satisfying instead of arbitrary.

### 3.3 The linear dig chain (Zroni: 6 sites, Baol/Grunur: 5+)

Each site's crystal or record points to the next site (coordinates). Every step tells **one chapter
of a history in order**: discovery of the Shroud, spreading out, the cost (feeding the galactic
black hole), civil war (Divine against Saviors), the secret weapon (Zro dust is Zroni bodies), the
final sacrifice. The option text celebrates the reveal ("The Dust... is the Zroni!"). It ends with a
reward and a hook (a crystal that seems sapient asks to come along).

**The second chain teaches through the enemy's records.** Baol history is learned from Grunur war
datacrystals, so the narrator is the conqueror, and the player rebuilds the victim's story from
fossils and biocomputer schoolbooks. This is two sources with a bias, done simply.

### 3.4 The three-chapter dig site with a randomised truth

"Sifting Through the Wreckage" / "Records Discovered" / "Fate Uncovered" / "Finding the Truth":

1. A ruined world was obsessed with being watched by aliens.
2. Its economy collapsed under anti-UFO spending, and an "ultimate shield" is mentioned more and
   more often.
3. The "shield" was killing their own people so no invader could take them, and it was set off by
   an "alien signal" from a nearby world.
4. Go to that world. The truth is **rolled**: either the aliens meant peaceful contact (tragic
   irony) or they planned to farm the population for food (they were right to be afraid).

This is exactly our `truths` mechanic, done in four short events.

### 3.5 The escalating chain (Horizon Signal: about 15-20 events over decades)

Repeat a motif with variation: three signals, three scientists called by name, three trips to the
Exit Point. Each repeat **escalates** (a poem, then a voice, then love poems) and each can be
**refused** ("This is a trick, or a trap. Log it and move on"), with a small consolation reward. Side
branches appear later and connect back: a temple on your homeworld, an escape pod holding a version
of your own species, a future version of one of your captains asking you to kill them. It ends in a
species-changing choice. The text never explains the Worm fully. It hands the player a
**working theory in plain words** in the final project ("a tangled knot of paradoxes that has become
conscious"), and leaves the rest to dread.

### 3.6 The crisis (Contingency: signal, symptoms, reveal, hunt, end)

1. A harmless oddity with a media nickname ("Ghost Signal").
2. Concrete symptoms with numbers: millions of synths missing, violence up tenfold, captured synths
   self-terminate.
3. Analysis gives a partial explanation (the signal looks like synth brain patterns).
4. Arrival: a corrupted, broken transmission, "CONTINGENCY PROTOCOL NOW IN EFFECT".
5. Hunt: four Machine Worlds open, each described as a physical spectacle (a gas giant burns like a
   small sun for a day, then cools to show a factory core).
6. Endgame: the central system, CX-9881.
7. Ending: a sober victory note. The flash of the last explosion will be seen as "a bright,
   short-lived star" for millennia.

### 3.7 Rewards and stakes summary

| Shape | Steps | Typical reward | Stakes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anomaly | 1-2 | Research, resources, modifier, ship | Low: failure costs time or a ship |
| Dig site | 3-6 chapters | Relic, minor artifacts, tech | Time; sometimes an awakened danger |
| Precursor hunt | 6-8 finds + reveal | Unique homeworld, big modifier | Race against other empires |
| Linear chain | 5-6 sites | Strategic resource source, relic | Moral choices; danger at the end |
| Escalating chain | 15-20 events | Species transformation, unique techs | Leaders die; your whole species can change |
| Crisis | 6-10 events | Survival, special resources | The galaxy |

## 4. Takeaways for Worlds Infinite

1. **Write fragments as reports, not aphorisms.** The default voice should be a named, competent
   observer saying what was found, where, and how they know: a royal surveyor, a temple archivist,
   a caravan master, a quarry foreman. "The chamber is forty paces across. Every skeleton faces the
   centre." Keep quoted verse and riddles for the few fragments that are folk songs or inscriptions,
   and even then add a line of plain context ("sung by net-menders on the east coast").

2. **Every fragment answers one small question and raises one bigger one.** Structure: fact, then
   specific detail, then the open question or implication. Never be vague about what a sentence
   means. Only be silent about what it implies. Spell out the known unknowns: "Nobody knows why the
   gates were bricked from the outside."

3. **Use numbers and named things.** Ages ("built 900 years before the realm"), counts ("212 bodies,
   all adults"), sizes, dates in the world's calendar, named people, named towns from the map. Our
   generator has all of these; fill them in. One number makes a fragment feel real.

4. **Give every big fact one small object.** A doll in a looted grave, a child's careful handwriting,
   a bell hung over a bed, a ledger with the last entry half-written. Have the generator keep a pool
   of these "intimate objects" for each premise and site kind.

5. **Make revelations plain cause-and-effect history with a sting at the end.** A `truth` should
   read like the Vultaum or First League reveals: what they believed, what they did, what it cost,
   and one ironic or sad last sentence. It should not be a mystical paradox. If a child could not
   retell it in three sentences, it is too cryptic.

6. **Scatter consistent clues so the player can guess the truth first.** As with every Vultaum site
   showing self-destruction, all the fragments and dig layers for one truth should share a visible
   pattern (every ruin was burned from inside, every grave faces the sea). Fragments for wrong truths
   or biased sources can bend the pattern, but the true pattern must show up in at least three
   independent places.

7. **Signpost the next step in plain words.** Each read or dig layer should end with a concrete
   pointer: "The ledger names a second workshop in {place}", "Coordinates for... the shrine at
   {place}". The journal should show a Stellaris-style quest line ("Find the Undercroft of {city}:
   the foreman's letter says it lies under the old drains"), with a counter ("3 of 5 sites of the
   Circle found") and map markers. The mystery should be in the findings, never in the instructions.

8. **Give premises a precursor-hunt shape:** introduce, scatter, converge. The first find gives a
   clear one-paragraph introduction (who, when, what they were like, one striking fact). Then 6-8
   scattered place-type finds (mine, observatory, colony, trade post, shrine, battlefield, splinter
   settlement), each with *what happened here* plus *one artifact, one new fact*. Then a final site
   that explains everything and pays out big. Add a race: a rival realm, cult or relic-hunter guild
   can get there first.

9. **Let sources have a bias the player can see, simply.** As with Baol history told through Grunur
   war records, or the Yuht museum of "famous alien frauds" that turns out to be wrong, label who is
   speaking and why they would bend it ("From the conqueror's campaign rolls..."). Bias should be
   about *what someone wants you to believe*, not about vague, garbled language.

10. **Put real stakes and rewards on every beat.** Each fragment, layer or storyline stage should
    change something we can show: a tech, a modifier, unrest, a unit, a map mark, a person's fate,
    a realm's relations. Show it like a Stellaris tooltip ("Reading this lets {realm} learn Field
    circles"). Show running stories as a Situation: stage, approach, and "an outcome is expected in
    about N years".

11. **Escalate by repetition with variation.** For storylines, use Horizon Signal's rule of three:
    the same motif comes back three times, stronger each time, and each time the realm or player can
    refuse for a small consolation. Name a specific person each time. Late side branches should loop
    back to earlier ones (the temple on the homeworld turns out to match the signal).

12. **One tone per text, many tones per world.** Keep each fragment or event to one register: dry
    humour, dread or wonder. Then mix registers across a world. Add humorous texture through
    institutions (guild disclaimers, a council's minutes, a tavern's price list, a bureaucrat's
    dismissive report), not through jokes inside horror.

13. **Give ancient powers explicit, enforceable taboos.** Like Fallen Empires ("abandon AI or we
    will MAKE you"), premise powers such as an old order, the god in the sea or the wardens should
    say out loud what they forbid, and the history simulation should act on it. That turns lore into
    stakes the player can see coming.

14. **Make flavour describe life, from more than one view.** Tech, building, subtheme and site
    descriptions should be one or two plain sentences about what changes for people. Let the same
    site carry different descriptions for different cultures (visitor centre or pilgrims' way, a
    plague pit or a holy grave). Let realms comment on world events from a pool keyed by values x
    attitude.

15. **Add a living-world drip.** Recurring, cheap, low-stakes updates, like enclave greetings and
    patron newsletters: a guild's yearly bulletin, a travelling troupe's news, rumours at the inn
    that change with the history simulation. Also add a chronicle timeline of plain dated
    milestones ("In 412, {realm} sealed the Undercroft of {city}"). This is what makes Stellaris feel
    like a specific galaxy rather than generic sci-fi: named recurring neighbours with their own
    small concerns.

### A before/after in our own material

Current (`world/sleeper.js`):
> "It is not angry with us. It does not know we exist. Each time it shifts in its sleep, a coast sinks, and that is all."

Stellaris-style rewrite (a report with numbers, an object, a known unknown and a pointer):
> From the harbour-master's survey of {place}, year 388: the old quay now lies eleven feet under
> water. It sank in a single night, the same night as the quays at {place2} and {place3}, 200 miles
> apart. No storm was recorded. Fishermen dredged up a bell from the drowned chapel; its clapper had
> been tied silent with rope. The survey asks the crown to send a scholar to the sea-caves at
> {place4}, where the night-watch reported "warm water rising".
